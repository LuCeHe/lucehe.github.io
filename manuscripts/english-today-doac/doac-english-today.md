Dots and accents: enough vowel signs for English, without a new alphabet

Abstract
English spelling takes children more than twice as long to learn as regular
systems such as Italian, because the letters do not keep one sound. The
International Phonetic Alphabet already writes the sounds, but most people
will not use a new alphabet or a new keyboard. This paper proposes DoAc,
short for dots and accents: the five familiar vowel letters, each with five
kinds of Hungarian-style mark, giving 25 vowel signs. That is enough for the
high British count of vowel phonemes. When two vowels are heard, as in night,
they are written as two letters (nait). If an r is heard, it is written. The
palatal glide is written i (yes is ies, yacht is iát, queue is kiú). Unmarked
vowels may be used for speed, but they are formally incomplete. Earlier
reforms left the Latin alphabet, ran out of vowel signs, changed too little,
or made children learn two systems. DoAc is a capacity argument and a working
map, not a teaching trial.

Keywords: English spelling; orthography; vowels; diacritics; literacy; spelling reform


English spelling is hard to teach because the letters often do not match the
sounds. One starts with a w sound, but we do not write w. Colonel sounds like
kernel. The letters ough are not one sound: they change from word to word, as
in though, tough, cough, plough, and through.

This is not a small classroom problem. Italian writing is close to one letter,
one sound: about 33 letter groups for about 25 sounds (Lepschy & Lepschy,
1981). English is full of exceptions. One count is about 1,120 letter groups
for about 40 sounds (Nyikos, 1988). In a study of 13 European writing systems,
children in regular systems such as Italian could read simple familiar words
well before the end of the first school year (more than 95 per cent correct).
English children were at 34 per cent in year 1. They needed more than twice as
long to reach the same level (Seymour, Aro & Erskine, 2003). Another study
found Italian children already reading about 94 per cent of real words at the
end of year 1 (Cossu, Gugliotta & Marshall, 1995). Spelling stays hard for
longer too: Italian children were accurate after two years of school, while
English children were still not accurate after five years (Marinelli et al.,
2015). The extra time is not because English-speaking children are slower. It
is because English letters do not keep one sound (Ziegler & Goswami, 2005).

The International Phonetic Alphabet already writes the sounds. Most people will
not use it for daily English. They want the familiar letters they already use,
without a new phone or laptop keyboard, and without a new alphabet. We propose
a small system that stays on those letters and adds marks to the vowels. We
call it DoAc, short for dots and accents. Five vowel letters times
five kinds of mark give 25 vowel signs. That is enough for the vowel sounds of
English. When two vowels are heard in a row, as in night, they are written as
two letters: nait.

The argument is a capacity argument, not a claim that English will change
tomorrow. Earlier reforms either left the Latin alphabet, or kept 26 letters
and ran out of vowel signs, or changed too little, or made children learn two
systems. DoAc is meant to avoid those four traps. We then give a working map,
show connected examples, compare the design with earlier reforms, and say what
the proposal does not yet prove.


The vowel problem, not the alphabet problem

English has many more vowel sounds than vowel letters. A standard account gives
about 20-25 vowel phonemes in Received Pronunciation, 14-16 in General
American, and 19-21 in Australian English (Roach, 2004; Wells, 1982; see also
the summary in Crystal, 2003). Some of the extra sounds are two vowels said
one after the other, such as the sound in night. Some are a vowel plus r, such
as the sound in here.

If we count only 'about 13' vowels, three kinds of mark on five letters would
already give 15 signs. That is enough for a squeezed General American inventory
if diphthongs are written as two letters. It is not enough if we want one sign
for each simple vowel and we also want to cover Received Pronunciation. Four
kinds of mark would give 20 signs, which is the low British number, not the
high one. Five kinds of mark give 25 signs, which matches the high British
count.

Linguists already have names for these vowel families. Each family is called by
one example word in capital letters (Wells, 1982). STRUT is the family of but
and blood. PALM is the family of father and yacht. TRAP is the family of cat.
Those names let us talk about the same group of words across accents, even when
the quality of the vowel changes. DoAc can keep the Wells groups as the shared
layer. The letters on the page then choose one pronunciation.

This split matters for teaching. Ziegler and Goswami (2005) argue that English
readers cannot rely on a single small unit, the letter, because letter-to-sound
maps are inconsistent. They also use rimes and whole words. Italian children
can stay with the small unit, because the map is stable. A reform that still
uses 26 unmarked letters, and then invents new piles of digraphs, keeps that
inconsistency in a new costume. A reform that invents 40 new shapes solves the
map, but not the classroom, because the shapes are not on the phone. The useful
middle is: keep the letters, mark the vowels, write two vowels when you hear
two vowels.


DoAc: Latin letters, Hungarian-style marks

Hungarian already writes a large vowel system with Latin letters and a few
stable marks. The full kit used here is:

| mark | letters |
| --- | --- |
| no mark | a e i o u |
| one dot | ȧ ė ị ȯ ụ |
| two dots | ä ë ï ö ü |
| one accent | á é í ó ú |
| two accents | a̋ e̋ i̋ ő ű |

Hungarian itself uses the double acute (two accents) because ö and ü can be
long. English may not need every cell. The point of the grid is that the
familiar five vowel letters, plus marks already used in Latin-script languages,
are not short of signs. People do not need a new alphabet in order to have 25
vowel symbols.

The marks are not a new keyboard. They are the same Latin letters, with
accents that phones already offer on a long press and that a United States
international or Hungarian layout already contains. That is different from
Shavian, Deseret, or the Initial Teaching Alphabet, which need extra shapes
and extra fonts.

When two vowels are heard, write two letters. Night is nait, not a special
single letter for PRICE. The 25 signs are for simple vowels. Gliding vowels
are two of those signs in a row: ai, au, ei, oi, ou.

Lower-case i already has a tittle, so the extra overdot on i is the awkward
cell. The grid lists ị, with the dot below, for that cell. In practice English
can leave it empty. Spare cells are a feature. They are how 25 signs cover a
range of 14 to 25 vowel phonemes without forcing every dialect to use every
mark.


A working map

Table 1 is one working map, not a national standard. Empty cells can be used
later, for example for the two different a sounds that some Australian speakers
have in bad and lad. The transcriptions are rhotic: if an r is heard, it is
written. Sure is shür, not shü. Some British speakers can drop that r. That is
their accent. It is also one reason General American is listed with fewer vowel
phonemes: it still says the r.

Consonants are the easy part. Silent letters are dropped. If the sound is k,
write k. The digraphs th, sh, ch, and ng can stay. So can s and z for the two
sibilants, and j for the sound in jeep. The palatal glide is written i, not y:
yes is ies, yacht is iát, you is iú. After a consonant the same i is used, so
queue is kiú and beautiful is biútifėl. The letter y is then spare. The letter
j is only the jeep sound, not the glide.
The letter c is not needed as a third way to write k or s. The letter q is not
needed beside u. The letter x can be ks. None of this is new. Cut Spelling and
New Spelling already cut silent letters. The hard part, and the part those
systems still under-supply, is the vowels.

**Table 1.** One DoAc map, using Wells (1982) lexical sets.

| Linguistic group | Word | Vowel | Transcription |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| STRUT | but | a | bat |
| PALM | yacht | á | iát |
| TRAP | cat | ä | kät |
| PRICE | night | ai | nait |
| MOUTH | now | au | nau |
| DRESS | bed | e | bed |
| FACE | day | ei | dei |
| COMMA | the | ė | thė |
| NURSE | colonel | ë | kërnel |
| SQUARE | hair | e̋ | he̋r |
| KIT | bit | i | bit |
| FLEECE | see | í | sí |
| NEAR | here | ï | hïr |
| LOT | cot | o | kot |
| GOAT | go | ou | gou |
| THOUGHT | thought | ö | thöt |
| CHOICE | boy | oi | boi |
| FOOT | book | u | buk |
| GOOSE | too | ú | tú |
| CURE | sure | ü | shür |

This map keeps LOT, THOUGHT, and PALM apart (kot, thöt, iát). Many Canadian and
American speakers merge some of those. A writer who merges them can use fewer
cells. The extra cells are for accents that still make the distinction, not a
claim that every English speaker has 25 vowels.

Should one accent be the standard? Canadian English could be seen as a strong
candidate. It is rhotic, so every hearable r is written, which matches the
rule above. It sits close to General American, already the main model in film,
pop music, and many classrooms. It is relatively uniform from coast to coast.
However, two familiar Canadian facts would hide splits that other large
accents still make. Many speakers merge LOT and THOUGHT, so cot and caught
would look the same, and PALM often joins them. Raising of PRICE before
voiceless consonants would also split night and ride, so they would no longer
share one spelling. Learners and dictionaries already name General American
or Received Pronunciation. The shared standard here is the Wells set, not a
national accent. The letters on the page then record one rhotic pronunciation,
close to General American, while still leaving cells for British distinctions.

START, NORTH, and LETTER are written as the vowel plus r when the r is heard:
car is kár, four is för, letter is letėr. FACE, PRICE, CHOICE, GOAT, and MOUTH
are two letters because they are two sounds.

A few extra Wells sets fit the same rule. BATH follows PALM in the
transcriptions here when the speaker has a long vowel (staff is stáf), and
follows TRAP when the speaker has a short one (stäf). CLOTH follows THOUGHT or
LOT according to the accent. happY is written í, like FLEECE, for speakers who
have a tense ending in happy. None of this needs a new kind of mark. It needs a
choice, stated once, about whose voice is on the page.


What the page then looks like

Once the vowels are marked, the famous hard words become ordinary strings.

(1) Though the tough cough and hiccough plough him through
    thou thė taf köf ėnd hikap plau him thrú

Those words look alike in ordinary English. In DoAc they have different vowels,
because they have different sounds.

(2) The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain
    thė rein in spein steiz meinli in thė plein

Rain, Spain, stays, mainly, and plain now share the same two letters, ei.

Further specimens:

colonel → kërnel
queue → kiú
women / woman → wimin / wumėn
one, two, eight, four → wan, tú, eit, för
knight → nait (the same as night)
choir → kwaiėr
yacht → iát
Leicester → lestër
beautiful → biútifėl
knowledge → nolij
yes → ies
I owe you → ai ou iú
here / hair / sure → hïr / he̋r / shür
cot / caught / cart → kot / köt / kárt

A connected sentence shows the same map at work, rather than a list of
curiosities:

(3) I told the colonel the choir would wait in the yacht.
    ai tould thė kërnel thė kwaiėr wud weit in thė iát

Ordinary function words are written as they sound: I is ai, the is thė, would
is wud. That is the point of starting from the sounds. It is also why a
phonetic page will look strange on the first day, and then look regular.

There is an old joke, often linked with George Bernard Shaw though the joke is
older, that fish could be written ghoti. The joke takes the gh sound from
enough (an f sound), the o sound from women (an i sound), and the ti sound
from nation (an sh sound). Put f + i + sh together, and you get fish (Crystal,
2012). The joke works only because the same letters change sound from word to
word. If each letter had one sound, ghoti could not mean fish. In DoAc, fish is
written fish.


Why earlier reforms fall short

Spelling reform is old. The attempts fall into a few groups, and each group
misses something that DoAc tries to keep.

Some people changed only a few words. Noah Webster gave American English color
and center (Venezky, 1999). That is easier to print, but the vowels are still
irregular. One, colonel, and ough did not get fixed.

Some people invented a new alphabet. Benjamin Franklin did this in 1768. The
Deseret alphabet did it in the nineteenth century. George Bernard Shaw left
money for a new alphabet with at least 40 signs, not based on Latin letters.
The result was the Shavian alphabet. One book was printed in it, Androcles and
the Lion, in 1962. Almost nobody used it. A new alphabet can match the sounds.
People still have to leave the letters they already know, and they need a new
font.

Some people kept Latin letters, but added extra shapes for school, then asked
children to switch back. The Initial Teaching Alphabet was used in British and
American schools in the 1960s (Downing, 1967). Children often learned to read
faster at first. Then they had to learn ordinary English spelling as a second
system. The extra letters were also hard to type. The method fell out of use.

Some people kept the 26 letters and respelt words, like thru and enuf. The
Simplified Spelling Society worked on this for many years, for example in New
Spelling (Ripman & Archer, 1948). Cut Spelling only drops spare letters, so
accommodation becomes acomodation (Upward, 1996). These look more like English,
but 26 letters are not enough for 20 to 25 vowel sounds. The extra sounds then
need piles of two-letter and three-letter groups, so the exceptions come back
in a new form.

So the old attempts miss at least one of these: they leave the Latin alphabet,
they do not have enough vowel signs, they change too little, or they make
children learn two systems. DoAc keeps the familiar letters, adds marks so
there are enough vowel signs, and is one system, not two.

Table 2 states the same contrast in one place.

**Table 2.** What each kind of reform keeps, and what it drops.

| Kind of reform | Latin letters | Enough vowel signs | One system for life |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Webster-style tweaks | yes | no | yes |
| New alphabet (Shavian) | no | yes | yes, if adopted |
| Initial Teaching Alphabet | partly | yes | no |
| 26-letter respelling | yes | no | yes |
| DoAc | yes | yes | yes |

The table is a design checklist, not a history of failure. Webster succeeded
because he asked for little. Shavian failed in use because it asked people to
leave the alphabet. The Initial Teaching Alphabet asked children to succeed
twice. New Spelling asked 26 letters to do 40 sounds. DoAc asks for marks on
the letters already in the font.


How it would be typed

A fair objection is that marks are slower to type than plain a, e, i, o, u.
They are. They are still not a new keyboard. On a phone, a long press on a
already offers á and ä. Windows and macOS already ship a United States
International layout. A Hungarian layout already has ő and ű. Unicode already
contains the precomposed Hungarian letters and the combining double acute for
the rarer cells. That is a different cost from Shavian, which needs a font
before the first word can be sent.

Writing with no dots or accents can be treated as fine for speed: thot for
thöt, the for thė. A reader who already knows the map can recover the vowels.
That page is still formally incomplete. The marks are what keep the vowel
families apart. A fast note can drop them. A primer, a dictionary, or a public
text should keep them.

In a classroom, the marks can be introduced as a reading aid first, the way
some dictionaries already print stress and quality. Children would not have to
throw the alphabet away in June and learn another one in September. That was
the ITA problem. DoAc is the same alphabet, with the vowel quality made
visible.


What would count as a test

We do not report a teaching trial. A first test would take people who are
learning to read English for the first time and split them at random into two
groups. One group is taught with DoAc. The other group is taught with ordinary
English spelling. After one day of lessons, both groups read the same list of
familiar words and simple invented words aloud. An evaluator who cannot see
the page, and does not know which group the reader is in, scores only from the
audio: which group reads more words correctly. That is closer to Seymour, Aro
and Erskine (2003) than asking adults whether they like the look of shür.
Until that trial exists, DoAc is a claim about signs, not a claim about
classrooms. The literacy numbers cited above show that English spelling has a
cost. They do not yet show that this notation removes it.


Limits

DoAc is a proposal, not a tested teaching method. We have not asked children to
learn it. Different accents will not pick the same marks for every word. People
who like old spellings will not like it. Morphology is sometimes hidden: sign
and signature would no longer look like the same family (sain, signėchėr),
which is a cost that strictly phonetic systems always pay. The same is true of
nation and national, of heal and health. Etymology on the page is a feature of
the present spelling. Regular sound is a feature of Spanish, Italian, German,
Finnish, and Hungarian. A reform cannot
maximise both.

Typing the marks is slower than typing plain a, e, i, o, u, even if it does not
require a new alphabet. Publishers would have to accept combining characters.
Spellcheckers would have to be rewritten. None of that is small. It is still
smaller than asking the public to learn 40 new shapes.

The claim is narrower. English does not lack symbols. Five letters and five
kinds of mark already give 25 vowel signs. That is more than the usual
schoolbook count, and it matches the high end of Received Pronunciation.
Children already spend years on the old spellings. The missing piece is not a
new alphabet. It is a writing system that starts from the sounds and stays on
the letters people already have.
